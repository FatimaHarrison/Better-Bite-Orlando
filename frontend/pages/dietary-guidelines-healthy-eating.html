Dietary Guidelines to Eat Healthy

Practical guidelines for building a balanced eating pattern and supporting healthy habits through every stage of life.

Building a healthy diet involves more than choosing individual foods. It includes paying attention to your body's needs, choosing a variety of nutrient-dense foods, and developing eating habits that can support your health throughout life.

The guidelines below cover everyday food choices, important nutrients, and considerations for different age groups and life stages.

1. Eat the Right Amount for Your Needs

  • The calories that you need vary depending on your age, sex, height, weight, and level of physical activity.
  • Pay attention to portion sizes, particularly for food and beverages higher in calories.
  • Remember to hydrate well. Choose water and unsweetened beverages over other drinks.

2. Prioritize Protein Foods in Every Meal

  • High-quality, nutrient-dense protein foods are part of a healthy diet.
  • Consume a variety of protein foods from animal sources (eggs, poultry, seafood, red meat) and vegetable sources (beans, peas, lentils, legumes, nuts, seeds, soy).
  • Choose cooking methods like baking, broiling, roasting, stir-frying, or grilling over deep-frying.
  • Consume meat with no or limited added sugar, refined carbohydrates or starches, or chemical additives. Flavor with salt, spices, and herbs.

Protein Serving Goal: 1.2–1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, adjusting as needed.

3. Consume Dairy

  • Include full-fat dairy with no added sugars. Dairy is an excellent source of protein, healthy fats, vitamins, and minerals.

Dairy Serving Goal: 3 servings per day as part of a 2,000-calorie dietary pattern, adjusting as needed.

4. Eat Vegetables and Fruits Throughout the Day

  • Eat a variety of colorful, nutrient-dense vegetables and fruits.
  • Eat whole vegetables and fruits in their original form. Wash thoroughly prior to eating or cooking.
  • 100% fruit or vegetable juice should be consumed in limited portions or diluted with water.

Vegetable and Fruit Serving Goals: For vegetables, 3 servings per day, and for fruits, 2 servings per day on a 2,000-calorie dietary pattern, adjusting as needed.

5. Incorporate Healthy Fats

  • Healthy fats are present in many whole foods such as meats, poultry, eggs, omega-3-rich seafood, nuts, seeds, full-fat dairy, olives, and avocados.
  • Prioritize olive oil, butter, or beef tallow when cooking with fats.
  • In general, saturated fat consumption should not exceed 10% of total daily calories.

6. Focus on Whole Grains

  • Prioritize fiber-rich whole grains.
  • Reduce the consumption of highly processed, refined carbohydrates, such as white bread, ready-to-eat or packaged breakfast options, flour tortillas, and crackers.

Whole Grain Serving Goal: 2–4 servings per day, adjusting as needed based on individual caloric requirements.

7. Limit Highly-Processed Foods, Added Sugars, and Refined Carbohydrates

  • Avoid highly processed packaged, prepared, ready-to-eat, or other foods that are salty or sweet, such as chips, cookies, and candy that have added sugars and sodium (salt). Prioritize nutrient-dense foods and home-prepared meals.
  • Limit foods and beverages with artificial flavors, petroleum-based dyes, artificial preservatives, and low-calorie non-nutritive sweeteners.
  • Avoid sugar-sweetened beverages, such as sodas, fruit drinks, and energy drinks.
  • No amount of added sugars or non-nutritive sweeteners is recommended for a healthy diet, and one meal should contain no more than 10 grams of added sugars.

Examples of Added Sugars

Look for ingredients that include the word “sugar” or “syrup” or end in “-ose”.

High-fructose corn syrup, agave syrup, corn syrup, rice syrup, fructose, glucose, dextrose, sucrose, cane sugar, beet sugar, turbinado sugar, maltose, lactose, fruit juice concentrate, honey, and molasses.

Examples of Non-Nutritive Sweeteners

Aspartame, sucralose, saccharin, xylitol, and acesulfame K.

8. Limit Alcoholic Beverages

  • Consume less alcohol for better overall health.
  • Pregnant women, people who are recovering from alcohol use disorder or are unable to control the amount they drink, and people taking medications or with medical conditions that can interact with alcohol should abstain from consuming it.

Other Important Notes

Sodium

Sodium and electrolytes are essential for hydration. However, the general population (ages 14 and above) should consume less than 2,300 mg per day of sodium. Highly active individuals may benefit from increased sodium intake, but this should be to offset sweat losses.

Daily Sodium Limits for Children Under 14

  • Ages 1–3: less than 1,200 mg per day.
  • Ages 4–8: less than 1,500 mg per day.
  • Ages 9–13: less than 1,800 mg per day.

Highly processed foods that are high in sodium should be avoided.

Gut Health

The gut contains trillions of bacteria and other microorganisms called the microbiome. A healthy diet supports a well-balanced microbiome and healthy digestion. Highly processed foods can disrupt this balance, while vegetables, fruits, fermented foods (e.g., sauerkraut, kimchi, kefir, miso), and high-fiber foods support a diverse microbiome, which may be beneficial for health.

Special Populations and Considerations

Infancy and Early Childhood (Birth–4 Years)

  • For the first 6 months of life, feed your baby only breast milk. When breast milk is not available, feed the baby iron-fortified infant formula.
  • At about 6 months of age, infants may begin to have solid foods. It is crucial to continue breastfeeding or formula feeding while solids are introduced. Breast milk or infant formula continues to be the main source of nutrition for your infant up to 12 months of age.
  • Introduce potentially allergenic foods—including nut butters, eggs, shellfish, and wheat—with other complementary foods at about 6 months. Ask your infant’s health care professional about their risk for food allergies and safe ways to introduce these foods.
  • Infants should receive a diverse range of nutrient-dense foods in appropriate textures, while avoiding nutrient-poor and highly processed foods.

Middle Childhood (5–10 Years)

  • Focus on whole, nutrient-dense foods such as protein foods, dairy, vegetables, fruits, healthy fats, and whole grains.
  • Avoid caffeinated beverages.
  • No amount of added sugars is recommended.

Adolescence (11–18 Years)

  • Adolescence is a rapid growth period with increased need for energy, protein, calcium, and iron—especially girls due to menstruation. Adequate calcium and vitamin D are vital for peak bone mass.
  • Adolescents should eat nutrient-dense foods such as dairy, leafy greens, and iron-rich animal foods, while limiting sugary drinks and energy drinks and avoiding highly processed foods.

Young Adulthood

  • Refer to Dietary Guidelines.

Pregnant Women

  • Pregnant women should consume diverse nutrient-dense foods, including iron-rich meats, folate-rich greens and legumes, choline-rich eggs, calcium-rich dairy, and low-mercury omega-3-rich seafood (e.g., salmon, sardines, trout).

Lactating Women

  • Breastfeeding women should consume a wide variety of nutrient-dense foods, including vitamin B12-rich protein sources such as meats, poultry, eggs and dairy; omega-3-rich seafood, folate-rich legumes, and vitamin A-rich vegetables.

Older Adults

  • Some older adults need fewer calories but still require equal or greater amounts of key nutrients such as protein, vitamin B12, vitamin D, and calcium.
  • They should prioritize nutrient-dense foods such as dairy, meats, seafood, eggs, legumes, and whole plants (vegetables and fruits, whole grains, nuts, and seeds). Fortified foods or supplements may be needed under medical supervision.

Individuals with Chronic Disease

  • Individuals with certain chronic diseases may experience improved health outcomes when following a lower-carbohydrate diet. Work with your health care professional to identify and adopt a diet that is appropriate for you and your health condition.

Vegetarians and Vegans

  • Consume a variety of whole foods, especially protein-rich foods, such as dairy, eggs, beans, peas, lentils, legumes, nuts, seeds, tofu, or tempeh.
  • Limit highly processed vegan or vegetarian foods that can include added fats, sugars, and salt.
  • Vegetarian diets often fall short in vitamins D and E, choline, and iron, whereas vegan diets often lack vitamins A, D, E, B6 and B12, riboflavin, niacin, choline, calcium, iron, magnesium, phosphorus, potassium, zinc, and protein.
  • To avoid nutrient gaps, monitor nutrient status periodically, prioritize targeted supplementation, and diversify plant protein sources.
